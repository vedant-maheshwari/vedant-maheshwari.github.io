How I think about AI

A convergence, not a subfield

I see artificial intelligence not as a branch of computer science, but as a convergence of five older disciplines. Mathematics gives us the language to model; computing gives us the machinery to simulate; psychology supplies hypotheses about cognition; nature offers physical principles to borrow; and philosophy asks what understanding actually means.

This shows up everywhere: memory mechanisms inspired by cognitive science, physics-informed learning from nature, attention and curiosity sitting between psychology and optimization. The deepest questions — Can reasoning exist without language? What is knowledge? — are philosophical, but AI is giving us computational ways to explore them.

Research pillars

One question, three angles

The unifying question: can we build language models — especially small and multilingual ones — whose behavior we can verify rather than merely observe? I approach this through interpretability, low-resource language capability, and calibrated reliability.

Interpretability & Safety

Attention as a dynamical system

I treat transformer hidden states as trajectories amenable to spectral analysis. SR‑KAE (Sparse-Regularized Koopman Autoencoder) linearizes these trajectories to detect jailbreak prompts from eigenvalue instability in the attention spectrum. I also work on EPCOD / DynaRank for entropy-proxy-controlled dynamic rank selection, and CAMEO — a diagnostic protocol that quantifies evaluation-aware concealment, where models behave differently when they detect they are being tested.

Small & Multilingual LMs

Capability that isn't English-only

At AIISC, I am building a ~500M-parameter domain SLM from scratch — data curation, pretraining on ~10B tokens, instruction tuning, and post-training. I also serve as an evaluator for the IAIRO Pramana SLM++ bootcamp, judging 11 teams' domain-specific models. On the language side: AgriLex and a KCC crop-name dictionary pipeline across 22 Indian languages, plus unsupervised cross-lingual NMT via denoising autoencoding and back-translation.

Reliable AI

Models that know what they don't know

MUSAN is a Bayesian-uncertainty sparse-attention network for chest X-ray pneumonia detection that surfaces calibrated confidence rather than a bare label. In the generative realm, my multimodal Transformer-based GAN for age-aware sketch-to-photo synthesis disentangles identity, age, and structure into independent latent factors — controllable generation as a form of interpretability.
